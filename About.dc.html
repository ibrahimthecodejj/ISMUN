About — Ignite School MUN 2026

ISMUN 2026 is the first Model UN conference Ignite School has ever run. It started with a handful of students who had been delegates elsewhere, came back frustrated that their own school had nothing like it, and decided to build one. The ambition is simple: a conference other schools actually want to travel to.

We run ISMUN because arguing well is a skill worth teaching properly. Delegates get real rules of procedure, topics with genuine disagreement inside them, and chairs who expect evidence rather than volume. The standard is straightforward: know your country's position, represent it honestly even when you disagree with it, and change your mind in public when someone out-argues you.

We are planning for a full weekend across six committees, from the Security Council to a historical crisis room, with a General Assembly plenary to close. Invitations are open to schools across the region and to individual delegates with no delegation behind them. Everything is being built to run properly the first time, not to be apologised for afterwards.

A first conference is a good one to join. Nothing is settled, no one has a reputation to defend, and the room is unusually forgiving of a delegate finding their feet. If you have never done MUN before, this is the conference to start at — a fair share of the people in your committee will be starting too, and the chairs are briefed to expect it.

This year's theme

From Spark to Settlement

Every topic on this year's agenda began as a spark — a shipping lane closed, a pledge unpaid, a technology outrunning its rules. Getting from there to a settlement the room can live with is the actual work of diplomacy, and it is slower and less satisfying than a speech. Committees have been briefed to reward delegates who move the room, not delegates who dominate it.

Welcome

A letter from the Secretary-General

Most welcome letters open by telling you how prestigious the conference is. This one cannot, because ISMUN has not happened yet. What I can tell you is what we have built: six committees with topics chosen because reasonable people genuinely disagree about them, chairs who have been trained rather than simply appointed, and a schedule with enough unmoderated time to actually negotiate instead of performing at each other.

The delegates who win rooms are almost never the loudest ones — they are the ones who read the draft resolution before objecting to it.

If this is your first conference, the honest version is that the first hour is confusing for everybody. You will not know when to raise your placard, and you will second-guess your opening speech. That passes, usually somewhere in the first unmoderated caucus, when you find two delegates who need what you have. Everything worth remembering about MUN happens after that point, so please stay past it.

Come with a country you have researched, an argument you can defend, and a willingness to be talked out of it. Bring the position paper, bring comfortable shoes, and bring the assumption that the delegate opposing you has read as much as you have. We will handle the rest. I look forward to gavelling in the first session.
